DeepSeek-R1 in early 2025 created a global shock: it proved reasoning capability could be achieved with open models and, along the way, made "Chinese open source" a world focus. But after R1, a question hung over the industry: how high can open models push reasoning? Alibaba's Qwen team gave its answer in late April 2025.

On April 29, the Qwen3 family was released. It ranged from 0.6B all the way up to 235B—the latter using a MoE architecture and being among the largest open models of its time. More importantly, Qwen3 natively supported both thinking and non-thinking modes: enable thinking for rigorous reasoning, disable it for fast response. Tool use and long context were all included, and all weights were open.

Qwen3-235B topped open-model leaderboards on many benchmarks, adding weight to the phrase "open-source flagship." But its significance went beyond the numbers. It turned the "open reasoning" pioneered by DeepSeek-R1 into a mature product line, showing the world with a full-size family that Chinese open models could not only build flagships but make reasoning standard equipment.

For developers, Qwen3 meant "flagship-grade reasoning model for free"—teams worldwide could fine-tune, deploy, and innovate on the 235B base. The remaining advantages of closed models were compressed again with each such release. Pricing pressure also spread industry-wide; anyone still using "unreachable" as a selling point looked increasingly out of date.

Looking back, Qwen3 is a milestone on Chinese open models' road to the top. From Qwen2's "serialization" to Qwen3's "flagship plus reasoning," Alibaba completed the turn from chaser to leader in two releases. When the world later debated "can open source actually catch up with closed source," Qwen3 had already answered with increasingly clear action.
