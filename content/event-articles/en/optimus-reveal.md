On September 30, 2022, a humanoid robot appeared on the stage of Tesla's AI Day—the Optimus prototype. The demo was modest: the robot walked on, waved, and moved slowly, with awkward, deliberate motions. But the signal the event sent was heavy: Tesla, the electric-car maker, was officially entering humanoid robotics, and claimed it would reuse its own motors, batteries, and FSD algorithms.

On the humanoid path, Optimus was not the starting point. For decades, companies like Boston Dynamics had built astonishing robots, yet most remained in labs and demos. What had always blocked humanoids was never whether the motions could be done, but cost, reliability, and generalization—mass-producing a robot that can work steadily is far harder than building one that can do backflips.

Tesla's high-profile entry pushed the track's heat to a new level. Musk's picture was seductive: robots moving boxes in factories, doing chores at home, eventually mass-produced at far lower cost than cars. That narrative pulled in capital and companies—Figure, Unitree, Boston Dynamics, and others charged ahead—turning humanoids from a lab niche into one of the hottest tracks in the AI industry.

Optimus's early demo also drew its share of criticism: the prototype moved slowly, had limited ability, and stood far from Musk's grand vision. But the criticism itself proved it was in the spotlight—shifting from "nobody cares" to "everyone argues" is itself proof a track is heating up. Whether robots truly enter homes and factories is still an open answer.

Looking back at Optimus's first reveal, it was like a starting gun, making "embodied AI" one of the loudest AI narratives after 2022. When Gemini Robotics wires large models into robot bodies in 2026 and major vendors race toward humanoid mass production, that slow-walking prototype of 2022 stands at the beginning of this boom.
