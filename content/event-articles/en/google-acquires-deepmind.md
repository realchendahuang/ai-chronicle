In January 2014 Google announced the acquisition of DeepMind. The price was estimated at $400–600 million, and DeepMind had been founded just four years earlier with almost no commercial revenue. Buying a lab with basically no products, for more than many public companies are worth—that alone was a clear signal of the capital logic of the AI era.

DeepMind started with heavy idealism. In 2010, Demis Hassabis, Shane Legg, and Mustafa Suleyman founded it in London with the slogan "solve intelligence, then use intelligence to solve everything." It specialized in combining deep learning and reinforcement learning, published a run of high-quality papers, and quickly became one of the most sought-after assets for investors in Silicon Valley and London. Reportedly both Google and Facebook bid; Google won.

The deal contained a telling detail: the two sides formed an external "AGI safety board," and DeepMind kept its London base and independent research identity. The arrangement answered the founders' public concerns about AI safety and provided institutional guarantees for research independence. It became a lasting talking point—how independent DeepMind really is inside Google remains a subject of external scrutiny.

After the deal, DeepMind's development exceeded almost everyone's expectations. AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol in 2016, AlphaZero dominated every board game with pure self-play in 2017, and AlphaFold cracked protein structure prediction in 2020. These results made DeepMind the world's most famous AI research institution and validated Google's original judgment: the lab was worth the price.

The deal's industry significance goes far beyond DeepMind itself. It declared a consensus—frontier AI research demands top-tier resources—and from then on Google, Microsoft, Meta, and Amazon all poured money into AI labs in wave after wave. The founding of OpenAI and Microsoft's investment unfolded under the same capital logic. The center of gravity of AI research began shifting decisively from universities to industrial giants.

Today the 2014 acquisition reads like a watershed: AI was no longer just an academic topic but a competition fought with real money among giants. DeepMind went from a small London lab to Google's ace, with personal ideals and corporate capital under one roof for nearly a decade—producing stunning results like AlphaGo while continuously fueling the "independence versus belonging" debate. By any measure, this deal rewrote the direction of AI history.
