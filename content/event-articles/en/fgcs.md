In 1981 Japan's Ministry of International Trade and Industry (MITI) issued a report announcing a fifth-generation computer. The project formally began in 1982 with an enormous goal: build knowledge-processing machines centered on logic reasoning within ten years—computers that could handle knowledge, understand natural language, and reason in parallel. The backdrop was Japan's dominance in semiconductors and industrial robots, and broad Western anxiety that if Japan industrialized "intelligence" as a commodity, the next computing era would be Japan's.

The technical bet was distinctive: PROLOG and logic programming at the core, organized around three pillars—inference machines, knowledge bases, and intelligent interfaces. Government led, joined by Fujitsu, NEC, Hitachi and others, with a budget in the hundreds of billions of yen. MITI founded ICOT (the Institute for New Generation Computer Technology), gathered many of Japan's best young researchers, and presented a national project in full siege mode.

The plan stung the West immediately. The United States launched the Strategic Computing Initiative; Europe launched ESPRIT. Three major powers wrote AI into national science strategy at once—before FGCS, AI had been mostly a university research topic. In that respect FGCS changed the game permanently: it made "AI competition" a national-policy issue, a frame that persists into today's compute race.

Actual progress was a different story. ICOT did build prototype inference machines such as PSI and PIM, and PROLOG research yielded solid academic results. But the core promise—intelligent computers that process natural language and reason about general knowledge—was nowhere near realized. As the bubble economy deflated and the AI winter deepened, the project quietly wound down around 1995. Critics later said it bet on the wrong route: logic programming did not become the foundation of computing, and connectionism returned with the deep-learning wave a few years later.

FGCS's legacy is double-edged. For the West it was an alarm that pushed the US and Europe back into AI research, objectively helping stockpile talent and projects for the AI revival. For Japan it was an expensive experiment, reminding later national programs that staking an entire industry on an unproven technological vision carries risk far beyond the budget. Today FGCS reads neither as a joke nor a monument, but as a real, costly national bet—whose wins and losses AI history keeps citing.
