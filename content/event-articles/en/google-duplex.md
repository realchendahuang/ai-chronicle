In May 2018 a clip at Google I/O silenced the room. It played a phone call: an AI called a hair salon, asked about appointments in a natural voice, dropping in "um" and "let me see" like a real person. The receptionist never suspected the other end wasn't human. The AI was called Duplex, and in that moment "AI making phone calls for you" stopped being science fiction.

To understand the weight of the demo, look at where voice assistants stood in 2018. They were good at "understand a command, give an answer". But in true dialogue—when the other person pushes back, reschedules, says "Thursday afternoon doesn't work"—machines fell apart. Duplex attacked the hardest part: holding fluent conversation through real, colloquial, unpredictable phone calls.

The most striking detail was the human texture. Filler words, pauses, hesitation—usually treated as flaws of speech synthesis, Duplex turned them into tools for sounding believable. That fusion of technology and design is what made it convincing. And that very believability raised the question: when a machine sounds exactly like a person, should it say so?

Google's answer was practical: the AI would identify itself at the start of calls, and Duplex would be limited to reservation scenarios. That compromise became a template for the industry—phone bots and customer-service AI largely adopted "state you're an AI first". Technically and ethically, Duplex was the pathfinder.

Looking back, Duplex proved that the value of speech AI isn't just understanding—it's getting things done. From Siri's answering to Duplex's doing, voice interaction crossed a threshold. Today's full-duplex voice models hold even more natural conversations, and that 2018 booking call is a start line they all trace back to.
