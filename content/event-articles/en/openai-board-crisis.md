On November 17, 2023, a message shook the entire tech industry: OpenAI's board had removed CEO Sam Altman. The announcement was vague, citing a "review process" that concluded he had "not been consistently candid." Soon after, co-founder and president Greg Brockman resigned, and chief scientist Ilya Sutskever became a focus. The world's most valuable AI startup had lost its leader overnight.

The next five days were dramatic. Employees organized fast, with more than 700 (the number later grew) signing an open letter demanding the board resign and Altman return, or they would defect en masse to Microsoft. Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella publicly welcomed Altman and Brockman, and reports even suggested the entire team might move. Meanwhile OpenAI's personnel, funding, and customer relationships shook violently.

Early on November 20 Microsoft announced Altman would join to lead a new AI research team—it looked like OpenAI's fate was sealed. But hours later the story reversed again: negotiations reopened. On November 22 OpenAI announced Altman would return as CEO, the board was restructured, and Sutskever left the board while keeping his company position. A crisis that might have ended OpenAI closed in five days with "founder returns."

The storm left more questions than answers. Why was he fired? No clear official explanation ever emerged; speculation centered on a dispute between safety and commercialization. Who truly controls OpenAI? The episode exposed the fragility of the "nonprofit parent plus for-profit arm" governance structure—a board that could fire a CEO proved nearly defenseless before employees and capital. The tension between mission-first governance and rapidly swelling commercial interest was laid bare before the world.

The industry impact was lasting. OpenAI's customers and developers realized for the first time that their API service could be interrupted by an internal personnel earthquake, and multi-model backup became enterprise standard practice. Investors re-examined AI companies' governance structures. And inside OpenAI, Altman reorganized the company's governance after his return—the earthquake did not end OpenAI, but it permanently changed its power structure.

Looking back at those five days, it reads like an early stress test: when a governance crisis erupts inside an AI company, how do employees, capital, and competitors act? The answer is now clear—employees rallied to defend the founder, capital applied pressure with an olive branch, and the founder closed the loop by returning. OpenAI survived, but the word "AI governance" was never again a paper concept—it had become a power struggle the whole world watched live.
