In October 2024 OpenAI added a seemingly routine but actually seismic feature to ChatGPT: web search. From that day, asking "what's the weather today" or "what's this stock at" no longer relied on stale training knowledge—ChatGPT browsed the live web itself and answered with cited sources. A chat assistant had formally entered the search market.

The crossing had been signaled for a while. In 2024 AI-search products like Perplexity had proven the answer-engine model—users no longer wanted a list of blue links, they wanted answers. OpenAI's move internalized the same capability into its core product. To do it, it struck copyright deals with news and data providers in advance, attempting to answer the industry's thorniest question: where does AI search content come from, and how do rights work?

ChatGPT Search feels completely different from traditional search. Traditional search gives you a list and you pick and click; ChatGPT Search gives you a polished answer with sources as small annotations. The shift from "list to answer" looks small but is a paradigm change in how people get information. Users want not to find, but to receive.

The impact was direct. Traditional engines monetize through clicks on ads, while AI search answers directly with no clicks needed. Google and Microsoft were forced to accelerate AI features, and Perplexity met its strongest rival. At the same time, ChatGPT Search pushed the "AI plus licensed content" model into the mainstream—future AI search must rest on profit-sharing with content creators.

Looking back at the ChatGPT Search launch, it marked AI companies attacking traditional search head-on for the first time. Today "ask the AI instead of searching the web" is a daily habit for many, and at the origin is that fall-2024 moment when search was built into ChatGPT—the two entry points that had tangled for decades finally converged.
