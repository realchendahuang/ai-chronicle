In October 2015 Tesla Model S owners received a software update, and their cars suddenly gained a feature called Autopilot: lane keeping, automatic lane changes, adaptive cruise. No new model, no redesign—an already-delivered car gained new driving ability through a code update. In the auto industry of 2015 this was almost unthinkable.

Back then the mainstream autonomy narrative was "one day there will be robotaxi fleets", with Google's test cars as the standard bearer. Tesla chose another path: ship driver assistance at scale first, let real drivers and real roads collect the data. Slower and far more controversial, but it produced something Google test cars never had—massive real-world driving miles.

Controversy arrived almost immediately. Autopilot was positioned as driver assistance requiring constant attention, but the name itself whispered "self-driving". Accidents followed, and the public debate over the boundaries of driver assistance began. Technically, though, it closed a loop: on-board sensors collect data, cloud models iterate, OTA pushes new versions, and the car grows smarter with every mile.

The impact reached far beyond Tesla. Traditional automakers faced "software-defined vehicles"; driver assistance became a core selling point; end-to-end driving data and OTA iteration became the standard thinking. That one OTA push in 2015 redirected the entire industry's R&D toward AI and data.

Looking back, Autopilot's greatest achievement was redefining how autonomy enters the world: not wait for perfection, but ship, then evolve. Whether Waymo's robotaxis today or the smart-driving race of Chinese automakers, a thread runs back to that software update in the fall of 2015.
