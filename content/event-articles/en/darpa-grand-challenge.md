In March 2004, in California's Mojave Desert, DARPA held its first Grand Challenge. Fifteen teams sent their unmanned vehicles onto a 142-mile course. The result was embarrassing: not a single car finished, and the best run covered about seven miles. Dust, crosswinds, and rough terrain shredded carefully tuned laboratory vehicles. Yet this "failure" is where the self-driving industry truly begins.

DARPA's motive for the race was blunt: use open competition instead of closed-door research to force usable autonomous-vehicle technology. The program began in 2001, the first race ran in 2004, and the rules were simple—whoever's vehicle could cover the course on its own won. No polished demos, no safety drivers behind the wheel, just real desert conditions. This "the real world is the exam" approach was the first time self-driving appeared as an engineering problem rather than an academic demonstration.

The turn came a year later. In the 2005 race, five vehicles finished; Stanford's Stanley, using lidar and machine learning, covered 132 miles in about 6 hours 53 minutes. From zero finishers to five in a single year was not luck—it was the entire perception-planning-control stack being forced into fast iteration by the desert.

The third race, in 2007, changed the venue: a mock city built on a decommissioned air base. Vehicles had to drive through blocks, avoid other traffic, obey traffic rules, and handle intersections—about 60 miles of continuous operation amid dynamic traffic. This round was no longer about "can you drive autonomously" but "can you drive autonomously under society's rules." Many later-famous self-driving teams emerged from or were inspired by these three races.

Looking back, the most precious thing DARPA's challenges gave us was turning "self-driving" from a distant idea into an engineering field with explicit rules, real environments, and public results. Today's Waymo, Tesla FSD, and the entire autonomous-driving industry can trace their earliest roots to those three races in the desert and the city. The failed first race was not shameful—it proved the road needed to be retried, and the way to retry it was to test technology on real roads.
