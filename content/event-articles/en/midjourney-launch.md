In July 2022 a seemingly unremarkable product began quietly going viral: Midjourney. It had no standalone web app and no mobile app; it lived as a Discord bot. Users typed /imagine plus a description in any Discord server, and seconds later a generated image appeared in the chat stream. That seemingly casual design became the key to its sweep across the internet.

Midjourney's popularity hit exactly two converging lines: technology and distribution. On the technology line, text-to-image had just matured in 2022—DALL·E 2 proved feasibility in April; on the distribution line, Discord offered what other products lacked: no new account, no waitlist, join a server and play. The result was that "AI art" spread virally for the first time—people around you were doing it; you could not avoid knowing.

Its style also set it apart. Compared to other text-to-image products of the time whose defaults leaned "photorealistic," Midjourney's output carried a distinct artistic feel—strong color, atmospheric lighting, close to illustration and concept-art texture. Users quickly found that with the same prompt, Midjourney's images "had more feeling." That aesthetic difference became its core advantage and drew in designers, game developers, and illustrators first.

Midjourney's business model was notable too. It used a subscription model from early on, charging monthly fees and generating solid cash flow without heavy marketing. Community galleries and contests built a massive creator community. This "product plus community plus subscription" combination has since been copied by countless generative-AI products—but in 2022 Midjourney was among the first to make it work.

Controversy arrived with it. Artists protested that AI trained on their work without permission, and Midjourney became a focus of lawsuits and public debate. The 2023 Dungeons & Dragons artist incident, the Getty Images lawsuit, and ongoing copyright discussions all put Midjourney at the center of the "legitimacy and originality of AI art" argument. It was simultaneously the most popular creation tool and the biggest target.

Looking back at Midjourney's launch, it proved two things: generative-AI product forms can completely escape traditional software frameworks, and democratization need not depend on flashy tech promotion—a bot hidden in a chat channel, with style and community, set the world on fire. It turned "AI art" from a technical topic into a cultural phenomenon, and placed the question "is machine-generated art really art" before everyone who had ever seen an AI image.
