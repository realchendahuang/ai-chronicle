In January 2025 OpenAI released a product called Operator. Its capability sounded like science fiction: an AI that opens a browser by itself, logs into sites, fills forms, and completes bookings—ordering food, tickets, shopping—without you touching anything. This was no longer chat where you ask and it answers; this was a general-purpose agent where you delegate a task and it runs errands for you.

The idea behind Operator is called a Computer-Using Agent: making the model behave like a person—look at the screen, move the mouse, type. It runs in a cloud browser, and every step is observable: what the AI is doing, where it is in the task, and you can stop or take over at any time. For sensitive operations like payments and logins, it has confirmation flows requiring your consent. That design of "observable, interruptible, confirmable" was the safety floor early agent products cared about most.

Two questions dominated the launch. First, could it reliably complete real tasks—the web is chaotic, with login walls, CAPTCHAs, and shifting layouts. Second, where is the risk boundary of letting an AI operate with your accounts? Operator's answer was "supervised autonomy": autonomous, but not blindly so.

Operator's significance was turning "agent" from a demo concept into a subscription product. 2025 was called the year of the agent, and Operator was the first mainstream service where "AI does my web tasks" became buyable reality. Manus and countless browser agents followed, and the industry narrative shifted from "models that chat" to "models that do".

Looking back at Operator's release, it opened a revolution in interaction: from "people interact with software" to "people delegate AI to interact with software". Today agents are everywhere, but at the origin is this product from January 2025 that could operate a browser itself—the first time "AI does it for you" became purchasable reality.
